Online donations and state registration

Does a Donate button trigger charitable registration?

A Donate button does not answer the question by itself. The real issue is how the nonprofit asks for donations, where donors are located, which states have registration rules, and which state filing requirements, exemptions, renewals, or recovery steps apply before a campaign expands.

Ask about online fundraising Check one state first

Signals that raise the question

  • Public appeals that invite donations from residents in multiple states.
  • Giving pages hosted on the nonprofit site, payment tools, or fundraising platforms.
  • Campaign traffic from email, social media, ads, events, or peer-to-peer fundraising.
  • Existing filings that may need renewal before a campaign expands.

A Donate button can be part of charitable solicitation

State charitable registration rules are generally focused on solicitation activity: asking the public for contributions. A donation button, giving page, or campaign link can matter when it is paired with public outreach, donors in regulated states, or an active fundraising campaign.

Questions to answer before relying on a Donate button

  • Where are donors, event attendees, sponsors, campaign audiences, and website visitors likely to be located?
  • Is the nonprofit actively asking for donations through email, social media, ads, peer-to-peer campaigns, or direct mail?
  • Are any state registrations already current, expired, delinquent, or missing?
  • Do exemptions apply, and are they automatic, filed, renewed, or state-specific?
  • Who owns confirmations, renewal deadlines, rejected filings, and public registry monitoring after launch?

Separate a passive website from an active campaign

NASCO's Charleston Principles are advisory internet-solicitation guidelines, not a uniform national statute. They distinguish a website that can complete a donation transaction from activity that specifically targets people in a state or produces repeated, substantial contacts. State law and regulator practice still control, so the principles are a research framework—not a safe harbor or a substitute for a state-by-state decision.

Page capability

Can a visitor complete a contribution on the site, through an embedded tool, or after a redirect to a payment platform?

Do ads, emails, event promotions, grant appeals, chapter communications, or page copy focus on people in a particular state?

Current state examples show why one national shortcut fails

A Donate button can produce different answers depending on the nonprofit's home state, whether the page targets residents, and what happens after a contribution. These current regulator examples illustrate the decision points; they are not a substitute for checking every state in scope.

Connecticut: domicile and follow-up matter

Connecticut's Department of Consumer Protection says a Connecticut-based organization using only an online Donate button must register there. Its guidance treats a passively discovered out-of-state page differently, but says later solicitation of that Connecticut donor changes the analysis.

Read Connecticut's online-solicitation guidance

Maryland: the Donate button is an express example

Maryland's Secretary of State lists a Donate button as an example of soliciting charitable contributions in or from Maryland and directs organizations to register before that activity, subject to the state's specific rules and exemptions.

Six-part Donate-button registration checklist

  1. 1. Map the fundraising footprint. Record the home state, campaign audiences, donor history, events, chapters, grant appeals, paid media, email lists, and peer-to-peer activity.
  2. 2. Classify the online activity. Document whether the page merely accepts a gift or is promoted through targeted appeals, follow-up messages, ads, or repeated donor contacts.
  3. 3. Decide state by state. For each relevant jurisdiction, record the filing trigger, exemption path, regulator, portal, forms, fee, attachments, and approval boundary.
  4. 4. Check solicitation disclosures. Confirm whether the giving page, email, receipt, or campaign material needs state-specific disclosure language before launch.
  5. 5. Preserve launch evidence. Keep approvals, exemption acknowledgments, filing receipts, portal confirmations, page screenshots, processor terms, and the date each decision was reviewed.
  6. 6. Calendar the next trigger. Assign renewal dates, donor-geography monitoring, campaign-expansion reviews, rejected-filing recovery, and public-status checks to a named owner.

Donation-page launch record

Keep one dated launch record that names the page owner, payment processor, campaign channels, intended audiences, reviewed states, registration or exemption decisions, required state disclosures, and the person monitoring donor geography after launch. Revisit the record when paid media expands, a new peer-to-peer tool is added, a national partner promotes the appeal, or donor activity materially changes.

Practical caution: a payment processor or fundraising platform does not automatically take over the nonprofit's own registration analysis. Confirm what the platform files, what it does not file, whose name appears in the solicitation, and which records the nonprofit can retrieve later.

FAQ

Does adding a Donate button automatically require state registration?

Not automatically. A Donate button can create a registration question when it is part of public fundraising activity, especially across states with charitable solicitation rules.

Do online donations count as fundraising?

They can. Giving pages, payment links, peer-to-peer campaigns, and platform-based fundraising may all be part of charitable solicitation activity.

What should a nonprofit do before launching a donation page?

Confirm likely donor states, current registration status, exemptions, required attachments, renewal ownership, and recovery needs before public outreach begins.

Does a fundraising platform handle every state registration?

Do not assume so. Review the platform agreement and campaign structure, identify who is making the solicitation, and document which registrations or reports remain the nonprofit's responsibility.

Does a home-state nonprofit need to register if its only online tool is a Donate button?

State rules vary. For example, Connecticut's current guidance says a Connecticut-based organization using only an online Donate button must register there. Confirm the rule and any exemption directly with the nonprofit's home-state regulator.

Can a passive donation page avoid every out-of-state registration?

Do not assume that it can. Some guidance distinguishes a passively discovered page from targeted or repeated solicitation, while other states use broader triggers. Review each relevant state's current law and regulator guidance.
